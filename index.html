Four things that stand out

She fixes root causes, not symptoms

When an Entity Framework problem surfaced, the easy move was to work around it. Instead she went after DbContext lifetime management properly — scoped versus singleton versus factory, materializing queries inside their using blocks, correcting async void to async Task. The class of bug disappeared rather than moving somewhere else.

She is not afraid of legacy systems

She owns a multi-layer service chain — Web API calling a facade service over WCF, calling inner SOAP leaf services — the kind of stack most developers avoid. She worked through dependency injection in WCF with custom instance providers, web reference regeneration, and cross-solution debugging so breakpoints actually resolve. That is unglamorous, high-value work, and she does it without complaining.

She designs for the edge cases before they bite

Building time-bound insurance coverage, she didn't just store a timestamp. She reasoned through clock skew between device and server, network latency, timezone handling for Israel Standard Time, and how large a grace window can be before it becomes a fraud opening — then deliberately kept it small. That is product thinking, not just coding.

She removes friction for the whole team

Nobody asked her to build a PowerShell GUI that turns a Jira ticket number into the team's GitLab branch, tag and CI trigger. She noticed everyone doing the same manual steps and automated them — then debugged the SSH authentication mess underneath it so it worked on everyone's machine.

How she uses AI

This is the part I can speak to most directly. Naama doesn't use AI as autocomplete. She uses it the way a good engineer uses any tool — with judgment about where it helps and skepticism about where it doesn't.

  • She interrogates the answer. When I propose an approach, she asks what breaks, what the trade-off is, and why not the alternative. She has rejected plenty of my suggestions, usually correctly, because she knew a constraint I didn't.
  • She builds AI infrastructure, not just prompts. A RAG pipeline in n8n over a document store with a vector database and embeddings — including debugging the unglamorous parts, like API query syntax and an embedding dimension mismatch.
  • She solves for her real environment. Corporate network filtering blocks cloud AI tools, so she deployed local models with an IDE integration instead. AI assistance that works inside the actual security constraints, not in spite of them.
  • She uses it to understand, not just to generate. Her highest-leverage use is comprehension — mapping undocumented legacy code and tracing change impact across service layers, which is where the time actually goes.

The engineers who will matter most over the next few years are the ones who can hold a complex legacy system in their head and use AI to move faster through it. Naama already does both.

Working style, honestly

She is self-directed and learns in public — she'll go and read the documentation rather than wait to be told. She cares about maintainability in a way that shows up in small decisions: typed configuration over loose JSON blobs, a structured validation map over scattered conditionals, a clean rollback path on a risky branch before she needs one. She asks questions until she understands, which occasionally means a longer conversation and consistently means fewer defects later.

She works in Hebrew and English, in a right-to-left product with all the bidirectional-text complexity that implies, and builds components that other developers on the team then reuse.
